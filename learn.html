Learn to read it

Six short steps. The examples are isiZulu, but the same parts write the other siNtu languages of the region. Each step follows the project's own teaching booklet [2].

1 One triangle, one syllable

A syllable is drawn as a single block, an ibheqe. Its shape is the vowel: which way the point faces, and whether the far side is closed. The project describes the shapes as mimicking the shape of the mouth [1].

isiZulu writes its five vowels with i, a, u and the two closed sideways triangles (its e and o). Languages that tell more vowels apart also use the open sideways shapes.

2 Where a mark sits is where the sound is made

Consonants are drawn onto the vowel. Sounds made with the lips sit at the point of the triangle, sounds made with the tip of the tongue cross its middle, and sounds made at the back of the mouth sit at its base. A straight line is a stop; a curve is a fricative, where the air keeps flowing.

The mark keeps its place when the triangle turns, so one small set of marks serves every vowel.

3 A line for voice, a dot for a held breath

A bare stop is said with a puff of air: the bar alone is isiZulu ph. Two small marks change it. A line from point to base adds voice. A dot inside means the puff is held back, as in isiZulu's plain p and its b. Try both.

The same two marks turn th into t and d, and kh into k and g.

4 A circle for the nose

A small circle on the point is a nasal. Alone it is m; a line or cross inside it makes n, ny and ng. Set on top of another consonant, the plain circle means that consonant starts through the nose, as in mb or nz. A nasal that is a whole syllable, like the m in umfana, fills the block as one large circle.

5 Clicks

The three clicks are drawn as an hourglass open at the bottom. They take the same marks as other consonants: bare has a puff of air (ch), a dot is plain (c), a line is voiced (gc), a circle is nasal (nc).

6 Read a line

This is how the project describes itself in isiZulu. Its site gives the English as “a writing system for southern african languages” [1]. Read it block by block.

Words for the parts

The script has its own vocabulary. The first group comes from the project's site and booklet. The second comes from Wikipedia and has not been checked against a primary source. English glosses in brackets are ordinary dictionary senses added here, and corrections are welcome.

From the project [1] [2]

izimpawu zesintu
The traditional symbols of the region that the script is drawn from.
onkamisa
The vowels.
ongwaqa
The consonants.
ingungu
The nasal circle. The booklet shows it beside a drum.
intombi
The triangle pointing up [young woman].
insizwa
The triangle pointing down [young man]. Wikipedia calls this shape isoka.
indoda
Two triangles meeting at their points, an hourglass [man].
umfazi
Two triangles joined at their bases, a diamond [woman].
imibala
Colours.

From Wikipedia [7]

ibheqe, amabheqe
A syllable block, and the plural. Also the isiZulu name for beadwork.
uphimbo
The voicing line, from point to base.
lerothodi
The dot inside the triangle.
ingungwana
The circle touching the point.
ingungwanyana
A solid dot at the point that marks a nasal vowel.
amaqanda
The syllabic nasals, circles that fill the whole block.
umkhonto
The open shape pointing up, the vowel u.
iphambili, imuva
The closed triangles pointing left and right.
motsutswa, motsutsena
The open shapes pointing left and right.
usontandane
An open shape pointing down, for a consonant with no vowel. This tool does not draw it yet.
